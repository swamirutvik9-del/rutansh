### When Progress Benefits Everyone Except the Person Doing the Work

Some of the most important work is done by people who receive very little recognition for it:

- A person builds something. Someone else receives the credit.
- A team works late. One person becomes the face of the achievement.
- Someone takes responsibility during a difficult period. Nobody remembers who stayed when things were uncertain.

**This happens more often than we like to admit.**

### The Visible Result Hides the Invisible Work

We naturally notice outcomes:

The finished building.  
The successful event.  
The company.  
The award.  
The publication.  
The achievement.  

But outcomes usually have a history.

Someone planned. Someone organised. Someone solved problems. Someone stayed when others left. Someone did work that nobody thought was important enough to mention.

**The final result may have one visible face. The work behind it may belong to many people.**

### Why Does This Happen?

Partly because credit is easier to attach to individuals than groups:

- Stories need a central character.
- Organisations need a spokesperson.
- Media prefers a name.
- Audiences remember faces more easily than processes.

This is understandable.

**But it can create a distorted picture of how progress actually happens.**

### The Person Doing the Work May Eventually Stop

Recognition is not everything. People can work without applause.

**But consistently ignoring contribution has consequences.**

If people repeatedly see that responsibility brings little trust, little credit, and little opportunity, they may eventually stop taking responsibility.

They learn: *“Why should I do more when someone else will receive the benefit?”*

That is not merely a personal problem. It can become an organisational problem.

### Fairness Is Not About Giving Everyone a Trophy

Recognising contribution does not mean pretending everyone contributed equally.

**It means being accurate:**

- Who made the decision?
- Who did the work?
- Who took the risk?
- Who supported the process?
- Who carried the responsibility when something went wrong?

These questions make success more honest.

### Leadership Should Multiply Credit

A strong leader does not need to own every success.

**In fact, one sign of leadership may be the ability to make other people's contributions visible.**

When people know that their work matters, they are more likely to take ownership again.

Credit can therefore become an incentive. So can the absence of credit.

### The Bigger Lesson

Progress is rarely as individual as it appears.

**Behind most achievements are networks of people, knowledge, infrastructure, opportunity, timing, and support.**

Recognising that does not reduce individual achievement. It makes the story more accurate.

And perhaps more importantly, it creates a culture where people are willing to contribute even when they are not standing at the centre.

Because sometimes the healthiest question after success is not: *“Who won?”*

but: **“Who made this possible?”**

What feels normal today shapes what becomes unavoidable tomorrow.
