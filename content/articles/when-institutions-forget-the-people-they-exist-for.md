### When Institutions Forget the People They Exist For

Every institution begins with a purpose:

- A school exists to educate.
- A hospital exists to care for patients.
- A company exists to create value for customers and stakeholders.
- A public office exists to serve a public function.

An institution may grow larger over time:  
More departments.  
More employees.  
More procedures.  
More paperwork.  
More rules.  

And somewhere along the way, something can quietly change:

**The institution can become more focused on maintaining itself than serving its purpose.**

### When Process Becomes the Priority

Processes are necessary. Without them, organisations become unpredictable.

**But processes are supposed to support a purpose.**

Imagine someone coming to an office with a genuine problem.

The response is: *“Your form is incomplete.”*  
The person fixes the form.  
Then: *“You need another document.”*  
They bring it.  
Then: *“You need approval from another department.”*  

Each step may technically be correct.

**But the original question remains: Did the process help solve the person's problem?**

### Institutions Develop Their Own Needs

Large organisations naturally become concerned with their own survival:

- Budgets must be managed.
- Records must be maintained.
- Employees must be coordinated.
- Rules must be followed.
- Risks must be controlled.

None of this is inherently wrong.

**But these internal needs can slowly become more important than the reason the institution exists.**

That is when bureaucracy becomes more than paperwork. **It becomes a mindset.**

### The People Inside Are Not Always the Problem

It is easy to blame an employee. Sometimes an employee genuinely behaves badly.

**But sometimes the person is simply operating inside a badly designed system.**

- If employees are rewarded for avoiding mistakes rather than solving problems, they may become cautious.
- If questioning procedures is discouraged, people may stop questioning.
- If speed is rewarded more than quality, shortcuts may appear.

**Institutional behaviour is often shaped by incentives.**

### Good Institutions Need Feedback

A healthy institution should be able to hear: *“This process is not working.”*

That does not mean every complaint should automatically change policy. It means complaints should be examined.

People closest to a problem often see things that senior decision-makers cannot.

**Feedback is useful because institutions can become blind to their own habits.**

### Purpose Must Be Remembered

Growth is not automatically improvement.

An institution can become larger, more organised, and more complicated while becoming less useful.

The real test is simpler:

**Is the institution still doing the thing it was created to do?**

- If a school has excellent paperwork but students are not learning, something is wrong.
- If an organisation has perfect meetings but cannot solve problems, something is wrong.
- If a public process is technically complete but people cannot access the service they need, something is wrong.

### The Question Worth Asking

Every institution should occasionally stop and ask:

**“If the people we exist to serve disappeared from the picture, would our work still make sense?”**

If the answer is yes, perhaps the institution has started serving itself.

Institutions are built to continue. But they are also built for a reason.

**Remembering that reason may be one of the most important forms of institutional responsibility.**

What feels normal today shapes what becomes unavoidable tomorrow.
